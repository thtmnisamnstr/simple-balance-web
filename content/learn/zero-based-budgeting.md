In a zero-based budget, every dollar you have is given a job: rent, groceries,
the emergency fund, next summer's trip. You keep assigning until the money you
haven't given a job to reaches zero.

## What it is

The name comes from business budgeting in the 1970s, when it meant building
each year's budget from nothing rather than from last year's. For a household
it means something simpler:

**Money coming in, minus every dollar you've assigned, equals zero.**

The zero is the money with no plan, **not** your bank balance. A zero-based
budget with $3,000 in checking might have $1,400 assigned to rent, $500 to
groceries, $300 to the car, $200 to fun, $600 to savings, and nothing
unassigned. The money's all still there. It's just all spoken for.

## Why it matters

**Unassigned money drifts.** Money without a job tends to get spent on
whatever comes up. Giving it a job before the month starts is what stops that.

**Savings becomes a job like any other.** In a zero-based budget, the
emergency fund isn't what's left over. It's a line you assign money to, the
same as rent.

**Overspending has to come from somewhere.** When groceries run $60 over, the
$60 has to come out of another job. That makes the trade-off a decision rather
than a surprise at the end of the month.

**It works on any income.** Because you only assign money you actually have,
it suits pay that changes from month to month.

## How to do it

### 1. Start with the money you have

Use what's in your accounts now, or this month's pay once it arrives. Budget
money that exists, not money you expect.

### 2. List the jobs

Fixed bills first, then the essentials that vary, then savings goals and
extra debt payments, then the things that make life worth living. Include
the bills that don't come every month, like insurance or holidays, by giving
them a little each month.

### 3. Assign until the unassigned number is zero

Go down the list giving each job an amount. If you run out before the list
does, the last things on it wait. If there's money left after the list,
give it a job too: extra savings, a debt, a goal.

### 4. Move money when reality disagrees

A category runs over. Take the difference from another one and write down
that you did. That's the whole skill of zero-based budgeting.

### 5. Assign new money when it arrives

A refund, a gift, a side job: it gets a job the day it lands.

## How to do it in Simple Balance

Simple Balance does zero-based budgeting with two things together: budgets
that carry from month to month, and a figure called **left to assign**.

**Turn on carrying.** On **Budgets**, when you set or edit a budget, check
**Carry what is left over into the next month**. That makes it an envelope:
what you don't spend this month is added to next month's, and what you
overspend is taken off it.

**Watch left to assign.** Once any budget carries, the page shows what's left
to assign: the money in the accounts your budget covers, minus what every
envelope has already claimed. Your aim is to bring it to zero by giving the
rest a job.

**Choose which accounts count.** Each account has **The budget is about the
money in this account** on its form. Leave it on for checking, savings you
spend from, and credit cards. Spending on a card empties an envelope even
though no cash has moved yet. Turn it off for money that isn't part of the
plan, such as a retirement account.

**Move money between jobs.** When one category runs over and you want to cover
it from another, set a different amount for this month alone on both: press
the calendar button at the end of each budget's row. The usual amounts come
back next month.

**Cap what an envelope can hold.** **Most to carry** keeps an envelope from
growing forever, in both directions: the fund nobody draws on, and the
overspending nobody pays back.

from the other end, and [Budgets](/docs/budgets/) in the documentation says
exactly how left to assign is worked out.
