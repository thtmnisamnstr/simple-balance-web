Most budgeting advice assumes the same paycheck arrives on the same day every
month. If you freelance, work for tips or commission, run a small business, or
get paid every other week, it doesn't. You can still budget. You just plan
from the bottom up instead of the average down.

## Why it matters

**Budgeting from an average overspends in the lean months.** If you earned
$3,000 one month and $5,000 the next, planning to spend $4,000 is fine in a
good month and a shortfall in a bad one. The bad month is the one that puts
spending on a credit card.

**The good months disappear.** Without a plan, a big month feels like a raise
and gets spent like one. Then a lean month arrives with nothing set aside.

**Taxes can be a surprise.** If no tax is taken out of what you're paid, as
with most freelance work, part of every payment isn't yours to spend.

## How to do it

### 1. Find your floor

Look at the last six to twelve months and find the lowest month. That's your
floor: what you can count on.

### 2. Make a bare-bones budget that fits under it

Rent, utilities, groceries, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments.
The essentials should fit inside your lowest month. If they don't, that's the
first thing to work on, because no budgeting method can fix it.

### 3. Rank everything else

Write down what gets money next, in order: a buffer, savings, extra debt
payments, then wants. In a good month you go further down the list. In a lean
month you stop sooner, and you already know where.

### 4. Set aside tax first

If you're self-employed, put a share of every payment into a separate account
before you budget the rest. How much depends on where you live and what you
earn, so it's worth asking a tax preparer once.

### 5. Build a one-month buffer, then live on last month's income

The goal is a buffer equal to one month of spending. Once you have it, each
month's budget is simply what you earned **last** month. You always know the
amount before the month starts, because it already happened. Getting there
takes a few months of putting good-month money into the buffer.

### 6. If you're paid every two weeks

Every other week is 26 paychecks a year, so in most years two months have
three paydays instead of two. Budget each month on two paychecks, and treat
the third as extra: savings, a debt, or the buffer.

## How to do it in Simple Balance

**Record income as it lands.** Each payment is a **Deposit** with an income
category, such as Freelance or Tips. If you're paid on a schedule, set it up
once on **Recurring**: every 2 weeks works as well as monthly. Leave the amount
blank if it varies, and each payday proposes a row in **Staged** waiting for
the real number.

**Budget from last month's income.** On **Budgets**, under **Amount decided
by**, choose **A share of the income before it**. "Groceries get 15% of what
came in last month" then sets itself each month. It reads last month rather
than this one, because a share of a month still running would change every
time you looked at it.

**Rank what gets paid first.** Give your most important budgets a **Funded
first** number, where lower goes first. The budget report then shows how much
of each one the month's income actually covers, filling them in your order
until the money runs out. Rank rent and groceries first and the report tells
you, in a lean month, exactly which budget wasn't covered.

**Let a budget follow your history.** **What the last few periods spent** makes
a budget the average of recent months, which suits a category that moves
with your income.

**Look ahead.** The **What happens next** panel on **Budgets** projects your
balances month by month from what your accounts hold today, your recurring
transactions, and what you usually spend. A lean month shows up before it
arrives.
