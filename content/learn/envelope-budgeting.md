The classic version of envelope budgeting uses real envelopes. On payday you
take out cash and split it into envelopes labeled Groceries, Gas and Fun. You
spend from each one until it's empty, and then you stop. You may also hear it
called "cash stuffing."

## What it is

Suzanne Holloway, an extension agent at the University of Florida,
[describes the method](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/leonco/2025/09/12/budgeting-basics/)
as using "physical or digital envelopes for each spending category." Once an
envelope is empty, you stop spending in that category "until the next budget
period." In practice, that comes down to two rules:

1. **Each envelope holds the money for one kind of spending.** Grocery money
   comes from the grocery envelope and nowhere else.
2. **What's left at the end of the month stays in the envelope.** With cash,
   it's still sitting there, so it doesn't go back into one big pile. If you
   spent $340 from a $400 grocery envelope, next month starts with $60 already
   in it.

<Diagram name="envelope-budgeting-carry-over" />

The second rule is what sets envelopes apart from a budget that starts over
every month.

## Why it matters

The limit feels real. A number on a spreadsheet is easy to ignore, but an
empty envelope is hard to argue with. Even if you never touch cash, sticking
to "empty means stop" is what gives the budget teeth.

Spending less one month pays off the next. Because leftovers stay put, spending
less on gas in March gives you more for gas in April. In a budget that resets
every month, that saving just disappears.

You can see what overspending costs you. If you borrow from the Fun envelope
to cover groceries, you know exactly how much less fun you're having.

It also helps with costs that don't come every month. An envelope for car
repairs that gets $50 a month and sits untouched for a year has $600 in it
when the brakes go.

## How to do it

### 1. Choose which spending gets an envelope

Envelopes work best for spending that changes from month to month and that you
control, like groceries, eating out, gas, household supplies and personal
spending. Bills paid automatically, like rent or insurance, can stay in your
checking account.

### 2. Decide how much goes in each

Use what you've actually spent over the last few months.
[How to track your spending](/learn/track-your-spending/) shows you how to
find that out.

### 3. Fill the envelopes on payday

With cash, that means a trip to the bank. A Utah State University Extension
guide, [Dam that Cash Flow!](https://extension.usu.edu/archive/dam-that-cash-flow),
puts it simply: cash the check and put the money for each expense in its own
marked envelope. Without cash, you write down how much of your checking
balance belongs to each envelope.

### 4. Spend only from the right envelope

When one runs out, stop. If you really need more, move money from another
envelope on purpose and write down that you did.

### 5. Keep the leftovers

Don't sweep what's left back into one pile. Leave it in its envelope, or move
it to an envelope for something you're saving toward.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Cash has costs too">
Cash can be lost or stolen, and it earns nothing sitting in an envelope.
Paying bills online with it is awkward, too. You can keep cash envelopes for a
few everyday categories and use your bank account for the rest. Or skip the
cash and keep the envelopes as numbers.
</Callout>

## How to do it in Simple Balance

To use Simple Balance as an envelope budgeting app, make each envelope a
budget that carries over.

### Make a budget carry over

On [**Budgets**](/docs/budgets/), set a budget on a category and check **Carry
what is left over into the next month**. If you don't spend $50 of your grocery
budget in March, you have $50 more for groceries in April. Overspending carries
over too. It comes off next month's amount instead of being forgotten when the
month ends.

### Put a limit on an envelope

**Most to carry** limits how much an envelope can hold, in both directions. An
envelope you never touch can't grow forever, and one you keep overspending
can't sink forever.

### See what's left to put in envelopes

Once a budget carries over, the **Budgets** page shows what's **left to
assign**. That's the money in the accounts your budget covers, minus what your
envelopes already hold. It's like the cash you haven't put into an envelope
yet. Bringing it to zero is [zero-based budgeting](/learn/zero-based-budgeting/).

### Keep track of real cash

If you do use cash, add an account of type **Cash** for your wallet or your
envelopes. Taking money out at an ATM is a **Transfer** from checking to Cash,
since it's still your money. Spending it is a **Withdrawal** from Cash with a
category, and that's what counts against the envelope. In the list below, the
$600 moved from checking to savings is a transfer: it shows both accounts and
no category.

### Make cash quick to record

Cash never shows up in a bank file, so it's the spending most likely to go
missing. Save a [**template**](/docs/templates/) for cash purchases you make
often. Open the menu on any transaction and choose **Save as template**. Next
time, pick the template in the transaction form and it fills itself in. If you
leave the amount blank, it asks you for one each time.
