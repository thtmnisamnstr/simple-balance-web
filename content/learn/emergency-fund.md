An emergency fund is money set aside for things you can't plan for: losing a
job, a medical bill, a car repair you need to get to work. It's the difference
between a bad month and a debt you're still paying off a year later.

## Why it matters

**Emergencies go on credit cards otherwise.** Without savings, a $1,200 car
repair becomes a card balance, and the interest on it makes the repair cost
more every month it's not paid off.

**Many people don't have one.** In the Federal Reserve's survey of household
finances for 2025, 63% of adults said they'd cover a surprise $400 expense
using cash or its equivalent, which includes a credit card they'd pay off in
full. That leaves more than a third who would borrow, sell something or not be
able to cover it.
[The survey's summary](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-executive-summary.htm)
has the details.

**It protects everything else.** A budget, a debt payoff plan, or a savings
goal can all be wiped out by one bad week. An emergency fund is what keeps
them on track.

**It buys time to decide well.** Losing a job with three months of expenses in
the bank means you can look for the right next job rather than the first one.

## How much

The usual rule of thumb is **three to six months of essential expenses**.
Expenses, not income: what it costs to keep a roof over your head, the lights
on, food on the table and the minimum payments made.

Lean toward six months, or more, if:

- your income is irregular, or you're self-employed;
- yours is the only income in the household;
- people depend on you;
- your job would take a long time to replace.

Three months can be enough if there are two steady incomes and few fixed
costs.

**Start smaller.** Six months of expenses is a big number. Many plans start
with a first goal, such as $1,000 or one month of expenses, then build to the
full amount once higher-interest debt is under control.

### What counts as an emergency

Losing income, medical and dental bills, urgent car or home repairs, and
emergency travel. Not the holidays, a vacation, or the annual insurance bill:
those are predictable, and [sinking funds](/learn/sinking-funds/) are the
better place for them.

## How to do it

### 1. Work out your monthly essentials

Add up rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation and
minimum debt payments from a recent month.
[Tracking your spending](/learn/track-your-spending/) is how you get a real
number rather than a guess.

### 2. Multiply

Three times the monthly figure is your minimum, six times your fuller target.

### 3. Give it its own account

Keep it in a savings account separate from the one you spend from, somewhere
safe and easy to reach in a day or two but not so easy that it gets spent on a
whim.

### 4. Automate it

Set up a transfer on payday, even a small one. Add windfalls such as a tax
refund or a bonus.

### 5. Refill it after you use it

Using it is what it's for. Afterward, go back to step 4 until it's full again.

## How to do it in Simple Balance

**Find your monthly essentials.** **Reports** has an **Income and expenses**
report. Group it by month and read the expenses for the last three months, or
use **Overview** to see spending by category over the same range, and add up
the essential ones.

**Give the fund its own account.** On **Accounts**, add a **Savings** account
for the fund. On its form, turn off **The budget is about the money in this
account** so your budget doesn't treat emergency money as money to spend.
Moving money into it then lowers what your budget has left to assign, which is
the honest picture.

**Make the payday transfer recurring.** On **Recurring**, press **New
recurring transaction** and set up a **Transfer** from checking to the fund,
on your payday. Each time it's due, it proposes a row in **Staged** for you to
approve once the money has really moved. It never posts on its own.

**Watch it grow.** The fund's balance is on **Accounts**, and it counts toward
your **Net worth** report like any other account. When you do use it, record
a transfer back to checking, and the spending it covers in its own category,
so your reports show what the emergency actually was.
