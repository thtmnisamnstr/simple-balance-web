Your net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe. It's a single
number, and it answers a question a budget can't: over months and years, are
you getting ahead?

## What it is

**Net worth = what you own − what you owe.**

That's how [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investing-basics/financial-foundations), the group that oversees brokerage firms in the U.S.,
explains it too: add up what you own, then subtract what you owe.

What you own includes cash, checking and savings, retirement and investment
accounts, and things you could sell, like a home or a car. What you owe
includes credit card balances, student loans, a car loan, a mortgage, medical
bills, and anything else you're paying off.

Say you have $8,000 in checking and savings and a car worth $6,000. You owe
$2,000 on a credit card and $15,000 in student loans. You own $14,000 and owe
$17,000, so your net worth is **−$3,000**.

Don't panic if yours comes out below zero. Early on, a student loan can be
bigger than everything you own. What matters is which way the number moves.

## Why it matters

Net worth shows progress that a budget can't. A budget tells you about this
month. Net worth tells you whether the months are adding up to something.

<Diagram name="net-worth-trend" />

It counts paying off debt. Paying $500 off a loan doesn't feel like saving, but
your net worth goes up by the same $500 as if you'd put it in the bank.

It also catches slow leaks. Spending a little more than you earn each month
won't jump out of any one month's budget, but it shows up as a net worth that
slowly drops.

Your own trend tells you more than any comparison. Measuring your number
against a friend's, or a headline's, doesn't tell you much. Measuring it
against your own number from six months ago does.

<Callout kind="note" title="About those average net worth numbers">
The Federal Reserve's [2025 Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20261009a.htm), released
in October 2026, put the median family's net worth at $215,900 and the mean at
$1.24 million, both in 2025 dollars. The median is the family right in the
middle. The mean gets pulled up by a small number of very wealthy families, so
the median gives a better picture of a typical household. When you see a
headline about net worth, check which one it's using. Both numbers are per
family, not per person, and the [full report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf26.pdf) breaks them down by
age.
</Callout>

## How to do it

### 1. List what you own

Use today's balance for every account. For a retirement or investment account,
use what it's worth today. For a home or a car, use what it would really sell
for, not what you paid. If you're not sure, leave something like a car out. A
number that's a little low is more useful than one that makes you feel good.

Don't count everyday things like furniture or clothes. You can't easily turn
them back into money.

### 2. List what you owe

Use the full amount of every debt, not the monthly payment. That means the
whole credit card balance and the whole loan.

### 3. Subtract

Take what you own minus what you owe. Write the answer down with the date.

### 4. Do it again the same way

Once a month or once every three months is plenty. Use the same rules each
time, like whether the car counts, so the change from one date to the next
means something.

## How to do it in Simple Balance

Simple Balance works as a net worth tracker. Once every account is in it, one
report adds them all up for you.

### Add every account

On [**Accounts**](/docs/accounts/), press **New account** for each account you
have, including the ones that hold debt. For a debt, choose **Credit card** or
**Loan**. Type what you owe as a plain number and leave **Starting balance
type** on **Amount owed**. The account starts below zero, which is exactly how
it counts toward your net worth.

### More than one currency

If you have money in more than one currency, Simple Balance totals each one
separately and never converts between them. Any exchange rate it picked would
be out of date by tomorrow.

### Things it can't put a price on

Simple Balance doesn't look up what investments, a home or a car are worth.
You can keep them as accounts of type **Investment** or **Other asset**, but
their balances are only as up to date as the last value you entered. You can
also track just your cash and debts here and leave the rest out. That's a fine
net worth to watch, as long as you count it the same way every time.

### Accounts you've closed

You can archive an account you've closed from its row menu. Its history stays
there for you to read, and its balance stops counting toward your totals.
