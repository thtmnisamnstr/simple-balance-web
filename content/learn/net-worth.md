Your net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe. It's one number,
and it answers the question a budget can't: over months and years, are you
getting ahead?

## What it is

**Net worth = what you own − what you owe.**

What you own includes cash, checking and savings, retirement and investment
accounts, and the things you could sell, such as a home or a car. What you owe
includes credit card balances, student loans, a car loan, a mortgage, medical
bills and anything else you're paying off.

If you have $8,000 in savings and checking and a car worth $6,000, and you owe
$2,000 on a card and $15,000 in student loans, your net worth is $14,000 minus
$17,000: **−$3,000**.

That's a perfectly normal number for somebody early in their working life.
Student loans in particular push a lot of people below zero for years.

## Why it matters

**It shows progress a budget can't.** A budget tells you about this month. Net
worth tells you whether the months are adding up to something.

**It counts paying off debt.** Paying $500 off a loan doesn't feel like saving,
but your net worth goes up by exactly the same amount as if you'd put $500 in
savings.

**It catches slow leaks.** Spending a little more than you earn every month
doesn't show up in a single month's budget. It shows up as a net worth that
slowly falls.

**The direction matters more than the number.** Comparing your number with
someone else's says little. Comparing it with your own number six months ago
says a lot.

<Callout kind="note" title="About those average net worth figures">
The Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, released in October
2026, put the median family's net worth at $215,900 and the mean at $1.24
million. The gap is because a small number of very wealthy families pull the
mean up. Headlines about "the average American" often quote the mean, and the
median is the better picture of a typical household. Both are per family, not
per person.
</Callout>

## How to do it

### 1. List what you own

Use today's balance for every account. For a retirement or investment account,
use its current value. For a home or a car, use what it would realistically
sell for, not what you paid. If you're not sure, leave things like a car out
entirely; a number that's a little low is more useful than one that's
flattering.

Don't count everyday belongings like furniture or clothes. You can't easily
turn them back into money.

### 2. List what you owe

Use the full balance of every debt, not the monthly payment: the whole credit
card balance, the whole loan.

### 3. Subtract

What you own minus what you owe. Write it down with the date.

### 4. Do it again, the same way

Monthly or every three months is plenty. Use the same rules each time, such
as whether the car counts, so the change between two dates means something.

## How to do it in Simple Balance

**Add every account.** On **Accounts**, press **New account** for each
account you have, including the ones that hold debt. Choose **Credit card** or
**Loan** for debts and enter what you owe with **Starting balance type** left
on **Amount owed**. The account opens below zero, which is exactly how it
counts toward your net worth.

**Read the report.** **Reports** has a **Net worth** report that adds up what
your accounts hold and what they owe. Group it by month or by quarter to see
the trend rather than a single number.

**Each currency is its own total.** If you hold money in more than one
currency, Simple Balance totals each separately and never converts between
them, because any exchange rate it picked would be out of date tomorrow.

**Things it can't price.** Simple Balance doesn't look up the value of
investments, a home or a car. You can keep them as accounts of type
**Investment** or **Other asset**, but their balances are only as current as
the last value you entered. Tracking only cash and debts here and leaving the
rest out is a fine net worth to watch, as long as it's the same one each time.

**Archived accounts stop counting.** An account you've closed can be archived
from its row menu. Its history stays readable and its balance stops counting
toward your totals.
