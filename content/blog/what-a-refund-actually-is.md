You return a pair of boots. Forty pounds comes back. Where does it go?

Most tools record it as income, because money arrived. That is the wrong
answer, and the way you can tell is that it makes two separate numbers wrong at
once.

## The two numbers it breaks

Say you spent £40 on boots in March and returned them in April.

Recorded as income, March shows £40 of clothing spending that you did not
ultimately do, and April shows £40 of income you did not earn. Your annual
totals are now £40 too high on **both** sides. They still balance — income
minus expense is unchanged — which is exactly why nobody notices.

Ask either question separately and you get a wrong answer. "What do I spend on
clothes?" is overstated. "What did I earn?" is overstated. And the budget for
clothing is consumed by a purchase that was reversed.

## What it is instead

A refund is a **reversal of the original entry**, not a new entry of the
opposite kind. Money coming back into a spending category debits expense, so
the spending it reverses goes down. Money going back out of an income category
does the mirror.

<Callout kind="tip" title="The rule in one sentence">
A deposit credits income and a withdrawal debits expense **only when no
category contradicts it**. The category decides, not the direction.
</Callout>

That is why naming a category matters more than it looks. The direction of the
money tells you what happened at the bank; the category tells you what it
meant.

## Why this is hard to retrofit

A tool that has been recording refunds as income cannot fix its history by
changing a setting, because the information needed to tell a refund from real
income was never captured — both are "money in, filed under something".

The only place the distinction survives is the category, which is why it has to
be decided at the point the entry is written rather than inferred later.

## What it looks like in practice

Nothing. That is the point. You file the refund against the category you spent
it on, and the spending goes down by forty pounds.
