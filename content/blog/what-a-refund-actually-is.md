You return a pair of boots. Forty dollars comes back. Where does it go?

Most tools record it as income, because money arrived. That's the wrong
answer, and the way you can tell is that it makes two separate numbers wrong at
once.

## The two numbers it breaks

Say you spent $40 on boots in March and returned them in April.

Recorded as income, March shows $40 of clothing spending that you didn't
ultimately do, and April shows $40 of income you didn't earn. Your annual
totals are now $40 too high on **both** sides. They still balance, since income
minus spending is unchanged, which is exactly why nobody notices.

Ask either question separately and you get a wrong answer. "What do I spend on
clothes?" is overstated. "What did I earn?" is overstated. And the budget for
clothing is used up by a purchase you took back.

## What it is instead

A refund is **money coming back into the category it left**, not income. You
enter it as a deposit, the way any money arriving is entered, and file it
under the spending category: Clothing, here. Because a spending category runs
against a deposit's direction, the $40 lowers your clothing spending instead of
counting as income. Money going back out of an income category does the
mirror: it lowers that income instead of counting as spending.

<Callout kind="tip" title="The rule in one sentence">
A deposit counts as income and a withdrawal counts as spending **only when no
category contradicts it**. The category decides, not the direction.
</Callout>

That's why naming a category matters more than it looks. The direction of the
money tells you what happened at the bank; the category tells you what it
meant.

## Why this is hard to retrofit

A tool that has been recording refunds as income can't fix its history by
changing a setting, because the information needed to tell a refund from real
income was never captured. Both are "money in, filed under something."

The only place the distinction survives is the category, which is why it has to
be decided at the point the entry is written rather than inferred later.

## What it looks like in practice

Nothing. That's the point. You enter the refund as a deposit under the category
you spent it on, and that category's spending goes down by forty dollars.
